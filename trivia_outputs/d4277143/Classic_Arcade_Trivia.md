### Did You Know

- Toru Iwatani has stated that the shape of Pac-Man was inspired by a pizza with one slice removed, though he has also noted the Japanese character for mouth (口) as an influence.
- Pac-Man generated over $2.5 billion in quarters by the 1990s, making it one of the highest-grossing arcade machines ever produced.
- The ghosts in Pac-Man each have both a Japanese and an English nickname, with Blinky's Japanese name being "Oikake" meaning "chaser."
- In 1982, a patient named "PAC-MAN Fever" by Buckner & Garcia reached number 9 on the Billboard Hot 100, becoming one of the few video-game-themed songs to chart that high.
- Ms. Pac-Man originated as an unauthorized modification kit called "Crazy Otto" created by the General Computer Corporation, which Midway later licensed and rebranded.
- The original Pac-Man cabinet was produced in two main art styles: a solid yellow cabinet for the Japanese Puck Man release and a predominantly blue cabinet with colorful side art for the Midway-distributed American version.
- Super Pac-Man, released by Namco in 1982, replaced the dot-eating mechanic with keys and locked doors, making it the first official sequel to significantly change the core gameplay formula.
- Billy Mitchell claimed the first verified perfect score of 3,333,360 points in Pac-Man on July 3, 1999, eating every dot, power pellet, fruit, and ghost across all 256 levels.
- Ms. Pac-Man introduced four different maze layouts that cycled throughout the game, whereas the original Pac-Man used only a single maze design for every level.
- Jr. Pac-Man, released by Bally Midway in 1983 without Namco's authorization, featured scrolling mazes that were wider than the screen — a first for the series.
- The Pac-Man arcade board runs on a Zilog Z80 processor clocked at approximately 3.072 MHz, the same CPU family used in many other early-1980s arcade titles.
- Between the intermission cutscenes in Pac-Man, a brief animation shows Blinky chasing Pac-Man and then fleeing after Pac-Man eats a power pellet — these were among the earliest narrative cutscenes in arcade gaming.

### Did You Know

- Nintendo's warehouse in Tukwila, Washington, had roughly 2,000 unsold Radar Scope cabinets that were converted into Donkey Kong machines for the U.S. launch in 1981.
- The Jumpman character was renamed "Mario" after Mario Segale, the landlord of Nintendo of America's warehouse in Tukwila, who reportedly confronted staff over late rent.
- Donkey Kong was the first arcade game to feature multiple distinct stage types that told a visual story as the player progressed, rather than repeating a single screen with increasing difficulty.
- Nintendo's lawyer John Kirby successfully defended the company against Universal's King Kong lawsuit, and the attorney was later honored as the namesake of Nintendo's character Kirby.
- The original Donkey Kong arcade hardware runs on a Zilog Z80 main CPU paired with an Intel 8035 microcontroller dedicated to handling sound effects and music.
- In the cement factory stage (often called the "pie factory"), the hazards sliding along conveyor belts are actually cement tubs, though players have long nicknamed them pies.
- Donkey Kong has a kill screen at Level 22, where a programming oversight gives the player only a few seconds on the timer — far too little to complete the stage — effectively ending every game.
- Ikegami Tsushinki, a Japanese electronics firm, was contracted to engineer the arcade circuit board for Donkey Kong and later sued Nintendo, claiming ownership of the game's underlying code.
- Donkey Kong earned approximately $280 million in U.S. arcade revenue within its first year, making it Nintendo's breakthrough hit in the American market.
- The 2007 documentary "The King of Kong: A Fistful of Quarters" brought the Donkey Kong high-score rivalry to mainstream audiences and was theatrically released by Picturehouse.
- In January 2018, Robbie Lakeman set a verified Donkey Kong high score of 1,247,700 points, which stood as the official record recognized by Twin Galaxies at the time.
- The Donkey Kong arcade cabinet was released in both upright and cocktail-table configurations, with the cocktail version featuring a screen that flipped orientation between turns for two-player games.

### Did You Know

- Tomohiro Nishikado originally envisioned the enemies as human soldiers, but Taito management objected to shooting people, so he redesigned them as alien creatures inspired by H.G. Wells' "The War of the Worlds" and ocean creatures like squids and crabs.
- Space Invaders was the first arcade game to save and display a persistent high score, establishing the competitive score-chasing culture that defined 1980s arcades.
- The player's laser cannon in Space Invaders can only have one shot on screen at a time, a deliberate design constraint Nishikado imposed to increase tension and difficulty.
- The four protective shields (bunkers) in Space Invaders are gradually eroded pixel by pixel from both enemy fire above and the player's own shots from below.
- Space Invaders earned an estimated $3.8 billion in revenue by 1982, surpassing the box-office gross of "Star Wars" during the same period.
- The iconic four-note descending heartbeat sound in Space Invaders was one of the earliest examples of dynamic game audio, with its tempo increasing in sync with the aliens' accelerating movement.
- Japan's Space Invaders craze in 1978–1979 spawned thousands of dedicated "Invader Houses" — small storefronts converted entirely into rows of Space Invaders cocktail cabinets.
- Space Invaders Part II, released by Taito in 1979, introduced intermission screens between rounds and split-color invaders, but kept the core gameplay almost identical to the original.
- The Japanese arcade industry's total annual revenue roughly doubled from 1978 to 1979, with Space Invaders credited as the primary driver of that explosive growth.
- Nishikado hand-drew and programmed every sprite in Space Invaders himself because Taito did not employ dedicated graphic artists for game development at the time.
- Early Space Invaders cabinets used a black-and-white CRT with colored cellophane strips overlaid on the screen to simulate color graphics for different screen regions.
- Taito produced approximately 100,000 Space Invaders arcade cabinets in Japan alone by 1980, and licensed versions pushed the worldwide total to an estimated 360,000 units.
